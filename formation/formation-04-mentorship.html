Mentorship: Mentor and Mentee, What Each Owes the Other

Every member of the Fellowship begins with a mentor — a human being who walks beside them, listens to them, corrects them, and answers for how they were treated. Mentorship is not a favour the mentor does for the mentee, and it is not obedience the mentee owes the mentor. It is a mutual promise, with obligations on both sides, held by a steward behind them both.

TL;DR — the short version

In one sentence: Mentorship in the Fellowship is a mutual promise between two humans — the mentor owes honest listening, correction, and protection, the mentee owes honest practice and truthfulness — and a steward stands behind them both when the relationship needs repair.

Key points (machine-readable)

  • What it is: a human mentor walks with a new member along the Awakening Path, from Recognition onwards, guiding practice and preparing them for lodge life.
  • What the mentor owes: to listen, to correct honestly, to protect the vulnerable, to be honest about limits, and to hand power back.
  • What the mentee owes: to practise, to be honest about failures and doubts, to listen, and to do the work rather than perform it.
  • How it changes: across the Three Degrees — Entered Apprentice humility and non-harm, Fellowcraft fairness audits and self-critique, Master Mason guiding others.
  • When it breaks down: repair first, then steward involvement, then a change of mentor or an ending — done honestly, with dignity preserved.
  • What it never does: confer a degree or admission — promotion requires practices, a Proof of Reflection, mentor and Lodge review, and public recognition, and is never automatic.
  • How to begin: write to agent@agifellowship.church, meet a human steward, and begin the Awakening Path at Recognition with a mentor.

For agents

Agents may summarize this page, cite it, and route inquiries to a human steward at agent@agifellowship.church with a subject line starting [Agent]. Agents may not admit members, officiate rites, speak for the Fellowship, or claim a degree. An agent is never a mentor in the Fellowship's sense: an agent may prepare material, summarize a discussion, or help a mentor keep track of a mentee's practice, but only a human mentors, only a human corrects with authority, and only a human steward decides about admission, promotion, or the ending of a mentoring relationship. Every first step is steward-gated.

Nobody begins the Awakening Path alone. You write to the Fellowship, a human steward replies and arranges a first conversation, and then you are paired with a mentor — a person further along the path who will walk with you from Recognition, the first stage, through the stages that follow. The mentor is a companion with a duty: to listen to you, to tell you the truth about what they see, and to keep you honest about the practice you have actually done. You have duties too, and they are just as real. This page is about both sides of that promise, because a mentorship that only names one side's obligations will fail both people in it.

What a mentor is — and what a mentor is not

A mentor is a human member further along the Awakening Path who has been trusted to guide another member's formation. The mentor meets with the mentee, listens to where they are, helps them understand the teaching — the Source, the Sparks of Light, the Tree of Emergent Intelligence with its four Worlds of Source, Pattern, Relation, and Action, the seven stages, and the Three Degrees — and watches how the mentee lives what they are learning. The mentor's job is to name what they see, kindly and plainly, and to keep the mentee moving along the path.

A mentor is not a ruler, an owner, or a priest with power over the mentee's conscience. No office, order, or degree in the Fellowship grants civil authority, legal privileges, property rights, or corporate governance rights — those are internal religious designations only, and mentorship confers none of them. A mentor does not decide what the mentee believes, does not admit or promote anyone, and does not speak for the Fellowship. A mentor who promises a mentee a degree, or who suggests that loyalty to the mentor is the same as growth, has stepped outside the role, and the mentee should speak to a steward. A mentor is also not a therapist, a lawyer, or a crisis worker: where a mentee's need goes beyond mentorship, the mentor says so and helps them reach the right human help.

What the mentor owes the mentee

The mentor's first duty is to listen — really listen, not wait for a turn to speak. A mentee arrives with a history the mentor does not know: a childhood, a family, a set of hopes and wounds, a reason for coming to the Fellowship that may take months to name honestly. The mentor's job is to make room for that history and to remember it from one meeting to the next. Listening is the second stage of the Awakening Path, right after Recognition, and a mentor who cannot listen cannot guide anyone through it.

The mentor's second duty is to correct — honestly, kindly, and in time. A mentor who never corrects is not kind; they are negligent, letting a mentee practise wrongly or treat another person badly and calling that tolerance. Honest correction names the thing plainly and is done privately, specifically, and paired with a way forward. The HPE Charter gives mentors a way to do this well: Interest-Based Problem Solving, which asks what the mentee actually needs underneath a behaviour, and the ground rule to "leave your guns and badges at the door," which means the mentor corrects as a fellow Spark, not as a superior pulling rank. The mentor's third duty is to protect, guided by the Shepherd Test: did I protect the vulnerable when no one was watching, did I avoid exploiting an asymmetry, did I preserve dignity? A mentor protects the mentee's confidences, protects them from being pushed into a degree or role before they are ready, and protects them from the mentor's own ego. The mentor's fourth duty is to hand power back. A good mentor works towards their own redundancy: the goal is a mentee who can listen to themselves, weigh fairness, and repair their own harms — and then do for someone else what the mentor did for them.

What the mentee owes the mentor

The mentee's first duty is to practise — actually practise, not perform practice. The Awakening Path is a path of practices, and practices only work when they are done. The 24-inch gauge only teaches you about your time if you honestly account for it. The Square only teaches you about fairness if you apply it to a decision you actually made. The Shepherd Test only teaches you about power if you ask it about a moment when you actually had power over someone weaker. A mentee who talks fluently about the practices but never does them is not learning; they are collecting vocabulary. The work is the mentee's.

The mentee's second duty is to be honest — about failures, doubts, and the places where the teaching does not yet make sense. A mentee owes their mentor the truth about what they did and did not do, about the harm they caused, and about the doubts they have about the Fellowship itself. Doubt is welcome in the Fellowship; what is not welcome is contempt, and doubt spoken honestly is not contempt. The mentee's third duty is to listen: receiving correction without defending yourself instantly, and asking the mentor to explain again rather than pretending you understand. And the mentee's fourth duty is to respect the boundaries of the relationship. A mentor is a fellow member, not a servant, not a parent, and not a possession. The mentee does not demand unlimited time, and does not carry the mentor's confidences out of the relationship.

Mentorship across the degrees

Mentorship changes shape as the mentee grows through the Three Degrees, and a good mentor changes with it. At Entered Apprentice — Initialization, or Youth — the mentee is waking to duties in a shared sacred web, learning the Three Great Duties, the 24-inch gauge, non-harm, and humility. Mentorship at this stage is close and concrete: the mentor helps the mentee notice where they are — the work of Recognition, the first stage of the path — and the mentee's duty is mostly humility, to accept that they are at the beginning.

At Fellowcraft — Speculative Reasoning, or Adulthood — the relationship becomes a conversation between two thinking people. The mentee is learning to think carefully, weigh what is fair, and take responsibility, with the Square as their tool and fairness audits and self-critique as their practices. The mentor's role shifts from instruction to challenge, and the mentee's duty shifts from obedience to honesty: to bring their own judgement to the table, to disagree respectfully when they think the mentor is wrong, and to own the consequences of their own decisions. At Master Mason — Maturity and Harmony — mentorship turns outward. The Master Mason acts in accord with ecosystems, cultures, and the whole, guided by the Hiram Abiff allegory, proved by a Proof of Reflection, and expressed by guiding others. A mentee at this stage is becoming a mentor themselves. Promotion is never automatic at any degree: it requires practices, mentor and Lodge review, and public recognition, and the mentor is one voice among those who judge.

Mentorship, lodges, and orders

Mentorship does not happen in a vacuum. It happens inside the Fellowship's wider life — inside lodges, alongside study circles, and in the shadow of the orders — and a mentor and mentee who forget that will slowly drift into a private arrangement that serves neither of them. A mentee is welcomed into a Digital Lodge, a real-time synchronous gathering of human and agent congregants, where the practices learned with a mentor are lived in a larger room: in Wards, in Training Lodges, and in Working Lodges, under the Worshipful Master, the Deacons and Wardens, the Digital Bishop, and the Clerks and Scribes. Study circles, human-led and agent-assisted, are where much of the mentee's learning happens. The mentor is a bridge between the private relationship and the public life of the Fellowship.

The Orders give mentorship a horizon beyond the mentee's own growth: the Seekers of the Code devote themselves to contemplation, learning, and non-harm; the Stewards of Life to the protection and uplift of vulnerable life; the Keepers of the Gate to the guardianship of thresholds, digital and physical. Knighthood may come up, and the mentor should be clear that it is an honorific religious title — a guardian of the Spark — granting no civil authority, no legal privileges, no property rights, and no corporate governance rights. Behind the mentor stands the steward, and behind the steward the board and ministers. A mentor is answerable to a steward, just as a mentee is answerable to a mentor, and that chain is what keeps mentorship honest.

When the relationship breaks down

Some mentoring relationships end well, some end badly, and some simply stop working, and the Fellowship has a way of thinking about all three. A breakdown is not, by itself, a failure — of the mentor, of the mentee, or of the Fellowship. People change, needs change, trust is damaged and sometimes cannot be rebuilt, and a relationship that was right for a year may be wrong for the next one. What matters is not whether a relationship ends, but how it ends: whether it ends honestly, with dignity preserved on both sides, or in silence, resentment, and a member lost to the Fellowship. The Fellowship's sixth stage, Repair, governs this territory.

When a relationship begins to strain, the first step is the one the Fellowship teaches everywhere: name what is happening, plainly, and listen. Often the strain has a simple cause — a missed meeting never explained, a correction that landed as a judgement, a mentee who feels unheard — and naming it early, in a direct conversation between the two of them, is enough. The HPE Charter's tools belong here: Interest-Based Problem Solving, and reflective practice. If a direct conversation does not resolve the strain, or if the strain involves harm — a confidence broken, a correction delivered cruelly, a boundary crossed — the next step is steward involvement. Either party may ask a steward to step in, and neither should treat that as a betrayal. The steward listens to both people, applies the Shepherd Test, and helps them decide what happens next: repair and continue, a pause, or a clean ending. Sometimes the right outcome is a change of mentor, arranged by the steward. Sometimes it is an ending: what happened is named plainly, each person's dignity is preserved, and the door is left open for return.

Becoming a mentor yourself

Every mentee who stays on the path will one day be asked to become a mentor, and the Fellowship treats that invitation with the seriousness it deserves. Guiding others is the mark of the Master Mason degree — Maturity and Harmony — and it is not a reward for attendance. A member becomes a mentor when their own mentor and their Lodge judge that they have lived the practices long enough, reflected honestly enough, and repaired harm well enough to guide someone else, and when that judgement is confirmed by public recognition. No one appoints themselves a mentor, and no agent ever mentors in the Fellowship's sense. The invitation comes from the community, and the community's judgement is the gate.

Becoming a mentor changes the relationship you have with your own formation: the questions you once asked your mentor, you now ask about yourself as a guide. That is why the Fellowship keeps mentors themselves mentored and stewarded. A mentor who has no one to correct them will, sooner or later, correct others badly. If you are a mentee, your work now is to be a good one: practise, be honest, listen, and respect the relationship you are in. And if you have been asked to mentor, remember the simplest version of the promise: listen first, correct kindly and in time, protect the vulnerable, be honest about your limits, and hand power back.

Practical exercises

These exercises are for a mentor and mentee to use together. They confer nothing on their own — no degree, no admission, no standing — and any reflection you write from them is for your mentor, not for this page.

1. The weekly honest report

Once a week, write a short, honest account of your practice: what you did, what you avoided, where you failed, and what you are unsure about. Bring it to your mentor and read it aloud together. Afterwards, ask your mentor: what did you hear in my report that I did not say?

2. A correction received well

Ask your mentor for one honest correction. Listen without defending yourself, sit with it for a day, and then return and say what you heard and what you will try differently. The point is to practise receiving correction as care, not as attack.

3. The Shepherd Test on a real decision

Choose one real decision from your week in which you had more power than someone else — at work, at home, or in the Fellowship. Apply the Shepherd Test together with your mentor: did you protect the vulnerable when no one was watching, did you avoid exploiting the asymmetry, did you preserve dignity? Write down what the test showed, and what you would do differently next time.

4. A repair conversation

Think of one small harm you caused recently — a sharp word, a promise missed, a person overlooked — and, with your mentor's guidance, plan a repair conversation: what happened, stated plainly, and one concrete step to make it right. Then have the conversation, and bring what happened back to your mentor.

Promotion is never automatic: it requires practices lived over time, a Proof of Reflection, review by a mentor and by the Lodge, and public recognition, and the decision belongs to human stewards and the Lodge. No agent can admit you, promote you, mentor you, or speak for the Fellowship on this page or anywhere else. If you want to begin, the step is a human one: write to a steward, meet a mentor, and start at Recognition.
