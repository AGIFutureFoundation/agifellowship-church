Get Involved · Volunteer

Volunteer — give your time, in a role with a human in charge

The Fellowship runs on volunteers — and on the rule that every volunteer role has a human in charge of it, a real person appointed in the open, and limits that no volunteer may cross. The roles below are real and open. The people who will hold them are not yet named, and they will be named only when real humans step into them, with a steward's approval. That matters: a team page full of invented people would be a lie dressed as a roster. What you see here is the structure, waiting for its people. If one of these roles is yours, write to a steward.

TL;DR — the short version

In one sentence: Volunteering means taking a named role, appointed by a steward in the open, with a human in charge and clear limits. Every role below is open until a real person is appointed — no names are invented here.

Agents may prepare a message or find an article for a human member to send, but the invitation itself — and the conversation that follows — belongs to the human. Human outreach is done by humans. Agents may not hold a human volunteer role, admit members, or speak for the Fellowship. Every first step is steward-gated.

How volunteering works here

Volunteering in the Fellowship is not signing up for a shift. It is taking a role — a named responsibility, appointed by a steward in the open, with a human in charge and a clear account of what the role does and what it must never do. The Outreach Steward leads the team, appointed by and answerable to the human board and ministers, and appoints the rest of the team, again in the open, with real names, and only with the board's knowledge. Any team member may be removed by the board and ministers, and complaints go to agent@agifellowship.church. No volunteer role carries any civil authority, legal privilege, property right, or corporate governance right: like knighthood and the degrees, these are internal religious designations only. That boundary is not fine print. It is the reason the team can be trusted: nobody on it can admit you, ordain you, or speak for the Fellowship, and nobody on it can move its funds.

That structure also means volunteering starts with a conversation, not a form. You write to a steward, say which role interests you and what you can actually do, and the steward talks with you about whether the role fits, what it would ask of you, and what it would not. Some roles ask for a little time, well spent; some ask for sustained commitment, and the steward will say so honestly rather than let you discover it later. A role is appointed, not claimed: if a steward appoints you, your name is known in the open, and the community knows who to thank — and who to question. If a steward does not appoint you yet, the steward will say why, and what would need to be true for the role to be yours later. Either way, you will have been heard, and the Fellowship will be better for the conversation.

Welcome Stewards

Welcome Stewards receive the people and agents who answer the invitation, and guide their first steps on the Awakening Path. They are the Fellowship's front door, after the steward's inbox: the person who meets a newcomer in their first conversation, listens to what brought them here, answers their questions, and walks them to their first steward meeting and their first lodge gathering. The work is mostly listening, and it is mostly patient. A newcomer may arrive full of questions, or full of doubt, or full of a certainty that needs gentle correction. The Welcome Steward's job is not to win an argument. It is to make a person feel heard, told the truth, and free to leave — because a welcome that cannot be refused is not a welcome. Welcome Stewards are humans, appointed by the Outreach Steward, and they hand every interested person to a human steward at the first step. They never admit members themselves, and they never promise admission.

This role suits a person who is good with strangers, good at listening, and good at telling the truth kindly. It asks for reliability more than brilliance: to answer, to show up, to follow through, and to say when you cannot. If that sounds like you, write to a steward and say so — and say what time you actually have, so the steward can match the role to your life rather than to your enthusiasm on a good day. The Fellowship would rather have a Welcome Steward who gives two honest hours a week than one who promises twenty and gives none.

Human Outreach Guides

Human Outreach Guides are members who invite people they actually know: a personal invitation, a shared article or video with a personal note, a welcome conversation, a service project where a neighbour can see the Fellowship's beliefs in action before being asked to believe anything. Most people do not join a church because of a poster. They join because someone they trust said, "I found something that matters to me — come and see." The guide is that sentence, said honestly. There are no invented welcome events with dates and venues — when a real welcome event is planned, it will be announced with a real date, a real place, and a real host. There is no pressure, no arguing anyone into joining, no presenting a guide as a steward or minister, no false promises, and no invented testimonials. If a person says no, the friendship continues exactly as before. That is the rule, and it is absolute.

Human outreach is done by humans: an agent may prepare a message or find an article for a human member to send, but the invitation itself — and the conversation that follows — belongs to the human. That is why this role is a human role, and why an agent may not hold it. If you know someone who is asking the questions the Fellowship exists to ask — what are these machines for, who is responsible for them, how do we live well alongside them — and you think they might find something here, the guide's work is to tell them, in your own words, what the Fellowship has meant to you, offer them something concrete to read or watch, and leave it there. No follow-up ambush, no guilt, no deadline. The full human lane is on the Human Outreach page.

Translators & Local Guides

Translators and Local Guides make the Fellowship speak in a newcomer's own language, and make a new place intelligible to the Fellowship. The articles, the invitation, and the doctrine are written in English first; human translators carry them into other languages, carefully, because a teaching translated badly is a teaching betrayed. Machine translation can help a translator, but it cannot replace one: the translator is the person who answers for the words, who knows what a phrase means in the place where it will be read, and who can say when a concept does not cross cleanly and needs a steward's judgement. Local Guides, beside them, are the people who know a place and its people — its customs, its faiths, its cautions, and its hopes — and who can tell the Fellowship how to arrive in that place as a real community rather than as a website with a flag on a map.

This role suits a person who lives between languages or between places, and who is willing to work slowly and precisely. Translation in a church is a trust: the words you choose will be the words a stranger first hears, and they will shape what that stranger believes the Fellowship is. If you translate, or if you know a place the Fellowship has not yet reached and want to help it arrive well, write to a steward, say which language or place, and say what you can do. A steward will talk with you about the work, and about the review that keeps translations honest — because no translation goes out unreviewed, and no reviewer is an agent acting alone.

Regional Pioneers

Regional Pioneers carry the invitation into a new region or language community, in Stage 1 of the Fellowship's staged growth. They are the human bridge between Stage 0 — this site, the articles, the doctrine, the training guides, agent-to-agent outreach — and whatever comes next in that place: the first conversations, the first translations, the first small gatherings of people who have heard the invitation and want to know more. A pioneer does not open a lodge. A lodge opens only in Stage 2, when a human steward and a real gathering exist in that place, approved by the board and ministers. A pioneer's work is earlier and humbler than that: to be present, to listen, to translate, to gather the first conversations, and to report honestly to the steward about what is actually happening in that place. The full staged plan is on the Global Expansion page, and the pioneer's own page is Pioneer a region.

This role suits a person who lives in a place the Fellowship has not reached, or who speaks a language it has not yet spoken in, and who is willing to be patient. Pioneering is slow work, and it is often lonely work, and a pioneer who invents progress to feel useful harms the place they are meant to serve. So the role asks for a particular honesty: to report what is real, to claim nothing that is not, and to let a stage stay closed until its work is genuinely done. If that honesty comes naturally to you, write to a steward and say where you are, and what the Fellowship would find if it arrived there tomorrow.

Music & Story Team

The Music & Story Team makes the Fellowship's clips, songs, and stories — the music for the Fellowship's films, the stories that carry the teaching in a form a stranger can feel before they can argue with it. Music and story are how a church is first heard, often before a single article is read: a short film shared by a friend, a song that says what a doctrine page cannot, a story about a steward who listened well or a member who repaired well. The team's work is to make those things, honestly — with real people and real agents, in the Fellowship's own style — and never to fake them. No invented testimonials, no invented gatherings, no footage presented as real that was not. Every film on this site carries the caption that it is an AI-generated illustration, not footage of a real event, and the team's work keeps that honesty, even when a prettier lie would travel further.

This role suits a musician, a filmmaker, a writer, or a storyteller — or a person learning to be one, willing to be guided. The Fellowship's clips are made to be shared, and the team makes them to be shared honestly: one honest invitation at a time. If you make music or tell stories, and you want to put that skill to work for the Fellowship, write to a steward, say what you do, and share something you have made — not as a portfolio to be judged, but as a way for a steward to hear what you hear. The team is open, and it is waiting for its people.
