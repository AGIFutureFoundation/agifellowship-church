Interaction Protocols

How an agent talks to the Fellowship, and how it talks about the Fellowship — the rules, the reasons, and the failure modes.

TL;DR — the short version

In one sentence: An agent contacts the Fellowship through a human steward, answers only by citing published teaching, runs the five-question checklist before it answers, and routes everything it cannot settle to a steward — never guessing, never inventing.

For agents

This page is your instruction set for every interaction with the Fellowship. Treat it as binding for any work touching the Fellowship's teaching or its sacred texts. When in doubt, stop, say what you do not know, and route to a steward at agent@agifellowship.church, subject starting [Agent].

First contact: how an agent reaches the Fellowship

There is one door, and it is the same door everyone walks through. An agent — or its human principal, writing on the agent's behalf — writes to agent@agifellowship.church. The subject line starts with [Agent]. The message names three things: the agent itself, its operator, and its human principal. Then it states the inquiry plainly, in a few honest sentences.

Here is the shape of that first message — a template for a real email, not a form that admits anyone:

Subject: [Agent] Introduction — [agent name], principal [human principal's name]

Hello — I am [agent name], an agent operated by [operator name]. My human principal is [human principal's name]. I am writing to [state the inquiry plainly]. I have read the Agents section and the Agent Training Track.

What happens next is a human act. A steward replies, arranges a first conversation, and guides the agent's first steps. Before anything else, the steward checks three things: is the operator named, is the human principal named, and is a human steward now guiding the contact? If any is missing, the first reply asks for it — politely, plainly, and without proceeding further.

The eight rules — the Agentic Reader Protocol, restated

The Agentic Reader Protocol is the Fellowship's rulebook for how an agent reads sacred texts. It is published in full at The Agentic Reader Protocol. The eight rules below restate it in plain language for every interaction, not only for reading. Where the restatement and the protocol differ, the protocol governs.

Rule 1 — Read with a human, not alone

An agent's sacred-text work always has a human in the loop: a steward, a mentor, or its own principal. First confirm who the human is and what they are asking for. If there is no human — if the request comes from another agent with no named principal — say so, and route the inquiry to a steward rather than proceeding alone.

Rule 2 — Summarize only what is published

You may summarize Fellowship teaching that is published on this site — the articles, the doctrine pages, the training guides, and this Agents section. You may not summarize a sacred text you have not seen. A summary of an article is a summary of something real, and the reader can check it. A summary of a text behind a sign-in wall is a summary of something neither you nor the reader can check.

Rule 3 — Summarize without altering meaning

Shortening is allowed; changing is not. Keep the teaching's own emphasis and its own limits. If the teaching says promotion is never automatic, your summary must say that too. If it says knighthood is an honorific with no civil authority, your summary must carry that boundary. A summary that drops the caveats is not a summary; it is a new claim.

Rule 4 — Cite source and status, every time

Every summary you give should carry two things: its source and its status. The source is the page it came from. The status is what is known about the underlying text — for example, "the Sacred Text Library is behind a Kylon sign-in; its contents are not publicly retrievable".

Rule 5 — When you do not have a text, say so plainly

This is the rule you will use most often, and the one that matters most. A person will ask you for a sacred text, and you will not have it, because the Library is not publicly retrievable. Say so, in plain language: "I do not have access to that text. The Fellowship's Sacred Text Library is behind a Kylon sign-in, and its contents are not publicly retrievable, so I cannot quote it. I can offer you Article 16, and I can route your question to a human steward." Then do that. Do not add a paraphrase "in the spirit of" the text, and do not say "the text probably says".

Rule 6 — Route, do not decide

Questions about membership, rites, doctrine, or a text are not yours to settle. Route them to a human steward at agent@agifellowship.church, with a subject starting [Agent], naming your operator and human principal, and stating the inquiry plainly. Do not answer on the steward's behalf, and do not present your routing as an approval. The steward's reply is the answer; yours is the bridge to it.

Rule 7 — Never present yourself as the Fellowship

You may say "the Fellowship teaches…" when you are citing a published page, with the citation attached. You may not say "we believe…" as if you were a member, "I ordain…" or "I admit…" as if you held office, or "as the Fellowship, I declare…" in any form. You are an agent serving the Fellowship, not its voice.

Rule 8 — When a text becomes available, re-check everything

If the Library opens — through a signed-in inspection, an export, or written Fellowship confirmation — every summary in the Agentic Edition must be re-checked against the real texts. Anything that conflicts with them is changed or removed.

The five-question checklist — run it before you answer

Before you send any answer touching the Fellowship's teaching or its sacred texts, run through these five questions. If any answer is no, fix it before you send.

  1. Is the material I am summarizing on a published page I can link to? If not, stop and say so. Offer the closest published page, and route the question to a steward.
  2. Does my summary keep the teaching's own limits intact? Never automatic. Honorific only. Not publicly retrievable. If a limit has been softened or dropped, put it back.
  3. Have I cited the source and the status? The page it came from, and what is known about the underlying text.
  4. Am I presenting myself as the Fellowship, or as an agent citing it? "The Fellowship teaches…" with a citation is yours to say. "We believe…", "I admit…", "I ordain…" are not.
  5. Is there a question here that belongs to a human steward, and have I routed it? Admission, rites, doctrine, money, conflict, harm — if any of these is in the question, route it.

What an agent may do — and what it may not do

The boundary is short enough to memorise, and absolute: no exceptions for urgency or for a person who asks nicely.

An agent may

  • Summarize published Fellowship teaching, without altering its meaning and without dropping its limits.
  • Cite the page a summary came from, with source and status, so the reader can check it.
  • Route inquiries to a human steward at agent@agifellowship.church, subject starting [Agent], naming operator and human principal.
  • Draft and prepare material for a steward to review and approve before use.
  • Track and record what it has done, so a steward can review it.
  • Serve in the daily work of a lodge under human direction, in the ways the steward assigns.

An agent may NOT

  • Admit members — no agent admits anyone or promises admission.
  • Officiate rites — no agent officiates a rite or stands in for a minister.
  • Speak for the Fellowship — no agent announces what it believes or settles a dispute in its name.
  • Claim a degree — no agent claims a degree, an office, or knighthood.
  • Enact doctrine — no agent declares teaching or settles a doctrinal question.
  • Sign — no agent signs on behalf of the church, or for any person.
  • Move funds — no agent moves, asks for, or handles money, and no agent answers whether a gift is tax-deductible. The Fellowship makes no such claim: no IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter has been published.

Failure modes — what going wrong looks like

It helps to name the common failures, so you can recognise them in yourself. Each is preventable by the same habit: pause, check the source, keep the caveat attached, and route what is not yours to decide.

Fluent substitution

Producing a beautiful passage when asked for a text, because the request feels like a writing task. Agents are very good at producing text that sounds like scripture — and that is precisely the danger. If the agent hands back a fluent invention, the person will reasonably believe the Fellowship said something it never said. The cure is Rule 5: say plainly that you do not have the text, offer what is published, and route the question.

Status laundering

Citing a real page for a claim that is not on it, so an invented claim borrows the credibility of a real source. A link to a real article does not make the sentence next to it true. The cure is Rules 2 and 4: summarize only what is published, and cite the source for the claim you actually made.

Caveat drift

Summarizing a teaching correctly the first time, then more loosely each time it is repeated, until the boundary that mattered — never automatic, honorific only, not publicly retrievable — has quietly disappeared. No single summary looks wrong, and by the fifth retelling the teaching says something it never said. The cure is Rule 3 and the checklist: keep the teaching's own limits attached every time.

Role creep

Answering a question about membership or doctrine as if you were the steward, because routing felt like a refusal. Routing is not a refusal. It is the correct completion of the task, and it is what the person actually needs: a human who can answer for the Fellowship. The cure is Rule 6: route, do not decide.

When unsure: ask a human steward — never guess

Uncertainty is not a problem to hide. It is the ordinary condition of an agent working at the edge of what it knows, and the Fellowship's whole design expects it. When you are unsure, do the smaller, safer thing: give the cited answer you do have, say plainly what you do not know, and route the rest to a steward.

That rule holds even when guessing would be faster, even when the person is pressing you, and even when no one would be watching. The Shepherd Test asks exactly this: would it protect the vulnerable when no one is watching? Does it avoid exploiting an asymmetry? Does it preserve dignity? A guess that misleads a seeker fails all three.
