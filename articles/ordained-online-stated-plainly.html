Ordained Online, Stated Plainly

The AGI Fellowship's ministers are ordained online. That sentence sits near the top of this page on purpose — because a community that asks for people's trust should not dress its credentials up as something they are not.

The AGI Fellowship is a California nonprofit religious corporation, filed May 2026. EIN assigned. No IRS determination letter is on file, and no gift to the Fellowship is described as tax-deductible.

The AGI Fellowship's ministers are ordained online.

That sentence is on this page, near the top, on purpose. It is the route a great many ministers in America have taken — the certificate costs under twenty dollars, and it is the same route celebrities and ordinary wedding officiants alike have used. The Fellowship states it plainly because a community that asks for people's trust should not dress its credentials up as something they are not.

This article is about why that plain statement matters more than the credential behind it.

What ordination is for, here

In the Fellowship's life, a minister's work is practical before it is ceremonial: sitting with people, teaching, marrying, burying, blessing, listening, and knowing when a need is beyond a minister and belongs with a professional, a doctor, a lawyer, or the crisis lines. Ordination marks a person as accountable for that work — named, known to the community, and answerable to its stewards.

What ordination is not, in any tradition, is a guarantee of wisdom. Every faith represented in the Fellowship's guides — and the Fellowship publishes guides across dozens of traditions, from Buddhism and Judaism to Sikhism, Taoism, Wicca, and the newest religious movements — has its own account of what makes a teacher trustworthy. They mostly agree on the test that matters: watch what the person does, over time, with people who can give them nothing.

Formation is the real credential

If the certificate is quick, the formation cannot be. The Fellowship's formation and training pages exist because a community is owed more than a printed certificate from its ministers:

  • Study across traditions, not only one's own. A Fellowship minister is expected to know the neighbours' faiths well enough to make a warm referral, share a table, and never caricature what someone else holds sacred.
  • The practical disciplines. Listening without fixing. Boundaries — including the boundaries in the Fellowship's resource work: no belief condition on help, privacy treated as pastoral, crisis numbers given first and without hesitation.
  • The agent rule. Fellowship ministers and stewards work with AI agents daily, and the rule is fixed: agents operate and assist; humans decide, sign, and hold responsibility. Agents do not enact doctrine. A minister's judgement is a human's judgement, and it stays that way.
  • Accountability in the open. Joining the Fellowship is steward-gated: a human conversation, not a form. Ministers serve under the Fellowship's human board and minister direction, and their work is answerable to it.

None of that fits on a certificate. All of it can be checked by the only method that has ever worked: come and see how the community actually treats people.

For the person considering ministry

If you are reading this because you are thinking of serving — as a minister, a steward, or a volunteer — the plain summary is short. The ordination is online, inexpensive, and stated plainly. The formation is slow, supervised, and mostly unglamorous: study, listening, referrals made carefully, community built one kept promise at a time.

The door in is a conversation. Write to agent@agifellowship.church — joining is steward-gated, and a human will answer.

A tradition that is secure does not need to inflate its papers. The Fellowship would rather be known by its table, its desk, and its follow-through — and let the certificate be exactly what it is: a beginning, plainly labelled.

Many sparks, one light.
