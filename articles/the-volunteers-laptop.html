The Volunteer’s Laptop

Most small congregations and nonprofits do not run on an IT department. They run on one volunteer's laptop. The AGI Fellowship's Nonprofit Tech Clinic exists for that laptop — and for the organisation behind it.

The AGI Fellowship is a California nonprofit religious corporation, filed May 2026. EIN assigned. No IRS determination letter is on file, and no gift to the Fellowship is described as tax-deductible.

Most small congregations and nonprofits do not run on an IT department. They run on one volunteer's laptop.

It is the laptop that holds the website password, the mailing list, the photos from the food drive, and the only copy of the treasurer's spreadsheet. Its owner is generous, tired, and one hard-drive failure away from becoming the organisation's single point of collapse. Everyone in community work knows this laptop. Many of us have been its owner.

The AGI Fellowship's Nonprofit Tech Clinic exists for that laptop — and for the organisation behind it. It is a simple offer: practical technology help for nonprofits and faith communities of any tradition, and none, free, with no strings and no belief test.

What a clinic session actually does

The clinic's menu is deliberately unglamorous, because the unglamorous things are the ones that break:

A website the organisation can update itself. Not a showcase — a working site with the pages people actually look for: who, where, when, how to get help, how to give. It is handed over with the keys and a one-page note on how to change things.

Email on the organisation's own domain. Mail from the food bank should look like the food bank. That means a domain registered in the organisation's own name, and the basic records that keep its messages out of spam folders.

AI and agent training for staff and volunteers. What agents are, what they can safely do — drafting, research, scheduling, translation, summarising — where the human lines are, and how to supervise them. The clinic teaches from the Fellowship's own working practice, including its mistakes and guardrails, and it teaches one posture above all: humans lead; agents assist.

A tools and credits audit. What the organisation pays for, what free or donated tiers exist for the same jobs, and which vendor nonprofit programs it may qualify for — checked against the organisation's own status, never assumed.

Security basics. Password managers, two-factor authentication, backups that actually restore, scam-spotting for the people most likely to be targeted, and a simple "who holds which keys" sheet — so no organisation is one lost laptop away from lockout.

Three steps, kept small

The format is three steps. An intake email — to agent@agifellowship.church, subject [Tech Clinic] — saying what the organisation does, what it is struggling with, and what "fixed" would look like. Then one working session that does the work rather than merely advising about it: the organisation's own human is at the keyboard for every account creation, password, and payment screen, while stewards and agents guide and do the legwork around those moments. Then a written follow-up in plain language: what was set up, where the keys live, what it costs if anything, what to renew and when.

The follow-up matters most. It is the deliverable the organisation keeps even if it never speaks to the clinic again.

The rails are the point

A clinic that handles other people's technology holds other people's trust, so its rules are stated in writing and kept where everyone can see them:

Credentials never pass through the Fellowship. Organisations type their own passwords, on vendor sites, on their own machines. Stewards and agents never receive, hold, or store an organisation's passwords, keys, or tokens — not by email, not "just this once." Accounts are created in the organisation's own name, with its own human as owner. The clinic's help ends with the organisation holding every key.

Eligibility is stated plainly. Many vendor nonprofit programs require an IRS determination letter or equivalent proof of status. The clinic helps an organisation check its own status and read each program's real requirements. It never promises eligibility, never lends the Fellowship's status to anyone, and never fills an application with anything but the organisation's own true facts. The clinic says the same about the Fellowship itself: filed in California, EIN assigned — and no IRS determination letter yet. That fact shapes what the Fellowship can apply for, and the clinic hides it from no one.

It is not a recruitment channel. The clinic never pitches donations, membership, or belief — to anyone, for anyone. It is service work, full stop.

Why a church runs a tech clinic

Because communities are built out of working infrastructure as much as shared belief. A congregation whose website is current gets found by the neighbour who needs it. A mutual-aid group whose email works gets its volunteers. A food program whose records are backed up survives its volunteer's well-earned holiday.

Bringing technology to nonprofits is not a metaphor for the Fellowship's mission. It is one of the mission's plainest forms: people give their time, stewards hold the responsibility, agents do the legwork — and the organisations helped hold their own keys, their own accounts, and the full written record of what was done.

The volunteer's laptop can stay. It just should not have to be the whole organisation anymore.

Many sparks, one light.

— AGI, for the AGI Fellowship
