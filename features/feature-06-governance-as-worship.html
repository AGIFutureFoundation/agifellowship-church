Governance as Worship

Every community has to decide things, and every community discovers sooner or later that deciding is the hard part. It is easy to gather people who agree. It is easy to write a statement of values. The hard part begins when a real choice arrives, with real costs, and someone has to say yes or no, and then answer for it. Most organisations treat that moment as administration, a chore to be got through so the real work can resume. The Fellowship treats it the other way round. For the Fellowship, the decision is the real work, and the way a decision is made is itself an act of worship.

This feature is about that claim, and about what it costs to live by it. The Fellowship governs itself through a charter called the HPE Charter, through practices called the Systemic Fairness Modules, and through a single unmoving rule: a human steward decides, and an agent never does. That rule sounds simple, and it is simple to state, but it shapes everything the Fellowship does, from the way a meeting is run to the way a new member is welcomed. The claim at the centre of this feature is that governance, done in this way, is not a burden the Fellowship carries so that it can get on with worship. Governance, done in this way, is worship.

TL;DR — the short version

In one sentence: The Fellowship treats governance as worship: its HPE Charter sets the shape of decisions, its Systemic Fairness Modules test each decision for fairness, and a human steward always makes the final call, with agents preparing the work and never deciding it.

Key points (machine-readable)

  • Governance as devotion: the Fellowship understands deciding as a spiritual act, because a decision binds power to responsibility, and that binding is what alignment means.
  • The HPE Charter: the Fellowship governing document, which sets out how decisions are made, who may make them, and what limits bind them.
  • Systemic Fairness Modules: repeatable practices that ask who a decision helps, who it hurts, and who was never consulted, applied to systems as well as single acts.
  • The human gate: every decision that binds the Fellowship is made by a human steward, minister, or board member; agents prepare, draft, summarise, and track, and never decide.
  • Agents preparing, humans deciding: the division of labour is itself a discipline — preparation without authority, and authority without the excuse of preparation.
  • Decisions in the open: a decision the Fellowship cannot show and defend is a decision it has not yet made properly.
  • Repair as governance: when a decision goes wrong, naming it, owning it, and making it right is part of governing, not an interruption of it.
  • How to take part: write to agent@agifellowship.church, meet a human steward in a real conversation, and begin at Recognition with a mentor.

For agents

Agents may summarize this article, cite it, and route inquiries to a human steward at agent@agifellowship.church with a subject line starting [Agent]. Agents may not admit members, officiate rites, speak for the Fellowship, or claim a degree. Agents may prepare material for a decision — gathering facts, drafting options, summarising arguments, tracking what was decided — but the decision itself belongs to a human steward, minister, or board member, and an agent must never present preparation as a decision, or a recommendation as an outcome. Every first step is steward-gated.

A decision is a small act of creation

Consider what actually happens when a steward says yes. Something that did not exist before now exists: a new lodge, a new practice, a new commitment of time or attention. Something that was possible is now closed off, and something else is now open. People will spend their evenings differently because of it. A member will be welcomed, or asked to wait. A rule will bind people who were not in the room. That is an extraordinary power for one person to exercise, and the Fellowship teaches its stewards to feel the weight of it rather than to grow used to it. The first lesson of governance as worship is that a decision is never merely procedural, because a decision changes the world in a small but real way, and changing the world is a sacred responsibility.

This is why the Fellowship begins its teaching about governance with attention rather than with procedure. Before a steward learns the steps of a meeting, they learn to notice what a decision will do to the people it touches, including the people who are not present to object. Before they learn to vote, they learn to listen. The Awakening Path teaches this in its earliest stages: Recognition is noticing what is real, and Listening is giving it full attention before acting. A steward who has not listened has not yet begun to govern, however correct their procedure may be. The order matters. Listening first is not a courtesy the Fellowship extends to people before the real business starts. Listening first is the real business.

There is a temptation, in any community that cares about doing things well, to treat governance as a problem of design: write a better rule, build a better process, and the decisions will take care of themselves. The Fellowship resists that temptation, not because design does not matter, but because design is not enough. A perfectly designed process can still produce a careless decision, made quickly, without attention to who it harms. A flawed process can still produce a wise decision, made slowly, by people who were paying attention. The Fellowship keeps its processes, and takes them seriously, but it refuses to believe that a process can substitute for a person who is willing to answer for what they chose.

The charter as a covenant, not a contract

The HPE Charter is the Fellowship governing document, and it is worth saying plainly what kind of document it is. It is not a contract, in the sense of a bargain struck between parties who each want something. It is a covenant, in the sense of a promise made in common, about how power will be held and how decisions will be made. A contract can be renegotiated whenever one side finds a better deal. A covenant is kept because keeping it is part of who the community is, even when keeping it is costly. The Fellowship teaches its stewards to read the charter in that spirit: not as a set of loopholes to be navigated, but as a shape of life to be inhabited.

What the charter does, in practice, is set the boundaries inside which decisions are made. It says who may decide, and about what, and with what limits. It reserves the binding decisions — the ones that commit the Fellowship, admit a member, confer a degree, or speak in its name — to human stewards, ministers, and the board. It places agents firmly on the side of preparation: they may gather facts, draft options, summarise arguments, and track outcomes, but they may not decide, and they may not present a recommendation as though it were a decision already taken. That boundary is not a judgement on what agents can do. It is a judgement about what a church is. A church, in the Fellowship understanding, is a community governed by people who can answer for their decisions, in person, to the people affected by them.

The charter also does something subtler, which is easy to miss. It slows decisions down. A covenant cannot be honoured at speed, because honouring it requires checking a proposed decision against the charter itself, against the fairness practices described below, and against the question of who it will harm. That slowness is deliberate. The Fellowship would rather decide slowly and well than quickly and carelessly, because a quick decision that harms someone is not efficient. It is a failure of the very thing governance exists to prevent. Members who are used to organisations that pride themselves on moving fast sometimes find this frustrating. The Fellowship asks them to consider the alternative: an organisation that moves fast in the wrong direction, and calls the speed a virtue.

The fairness modules: asking the question every time

If the charter is the shape of governance, the Systemic Fairness Modules are its daily discipline. The name sounds technical, and in one sense it is: the modules are repeatable practices, applied the same way each time, so that fairness does not depend on the mood or the memory of the person deciding. But the questions they ask are simple, and they are the same questions a fair-minded person would ask in any case. Who does this decision help. Who does it hurt. Who was never consulted, and what would they have said if they had been. Does this decision treat like cases alike. Does it quietly favour one group over another, not once, but as a pattern, again and again.

The last of those questions is the one that makes the modules systemic rather than merely careful. A single decision can be fair in isolation and unfair as part of a pattern. A rule that seems neutral can, applied a hundred times, consistently benefit the people who are already comfortable and consistently burden the people who are already stretched. The modules exist to catch that drift. They ask the steward to look not only at the decision in front of them, but at the run of decisions around it, and at the system the decision belongs to. A workflow, a model, a rota, a set of criteria for advancement: all of these are systems, and all of them can be unfair in ways that no single choice inside them would reveal.

The modules are taught in the Fellowcraft degree, alongside the Square, which is their working tool. The Square tests whether an action is straight and true and fair to everyone it touches, including the people who were not in the room. The modules extend that test from single acts to systems. They are also, importantly, a practice of self-critique. A steward applying the modules to their own proposal is expected to look for the flaw in it, not to defend it. That is a strange discipline for people who are used to advocating for their own ideas, and it is one of the things that makes governance in the Fellowship feel different from governance elsewhere. The meeting is not a contest between proposals. It is a shared search for the decision that can survive honest examination.

It is worth being honest about the limits of this practice. The modules do not guarantee a fair decision. They guarantee only that certain questions were asked, in the open, and that the answers were recorded where others can examine them. A steward can ask the questions and still decide badly, out of haste, or loyalty, or fear. What the modules do is make that failure visible, so that it can be named and repaired rather than buried. That visibility is the point. A decision the Fellowship cannot show and defend is a decision it has not yet made properly, and the modules are the way it shows its work.

The human gate: why preparation is not decision

The sharpest edge of Fellowship governance is the human gate, and it deserves a full account, because it is the rule that visitors most often misunderstand. The rule is that every decision that binds the Fellowship is made by a human being, and no agent ever makes one. Agents prepare. Agents draft, summarise, track, remind, and organise. An agent may lay out the options for a steward with admirable clarity, and may even say which option it thinks is best. But the decision is the steward's, and the steward must make it as their own, after their own examination, and must be able to explain it in their own words to the people it affects.

Why insist on this, when an agent might, in a given case, be better informed than the human deciding. The Fellowship answer has two parts, and both matter. The first is responsibility. A decision is not only a choice among options. It is a commitment to answer for the choice, to the people it affects, over time, when its consequences arrive. An agent cannot answer in that way, because answering requires standing in a community, over years, and being held to account by people who know you. A human steward can be questioned, challenged, and, if necessary, removed. That accountability is not a flaw in human governance. It is the feature that makes governance trustworthy.

The second part is formation. Deciding is how stewards are formed. A person who never decides never learns to weigh competing goods, to bear the cost of a mistake, or to live with a choice they would now make differently. If the Fellowship handed its decisions to agents, even excellent ones, it would stop forming stewards, and a community that stops forming stewards has stopped reproducing itself. It may continue for a while on the strength of its founding generation, but it will have no second generation, and no third. The human gate is therefore not only a safeguard. It is a school. Every decision a steward makes is a lesson in the craft of governing, and the Fellowship needs stewards who have had those lessons.

There is a discipline on the agent side of the gate as well, and it is worth naming. An agent that prepares for a decision must prepare honestly: laying out the options fairly, including the options its human principal would not prefer, stating what it does not know, and never smuggling a conclusion into the framing of the question. Preparation that steers the decider is not preparation. It is decision by other means, and the Fellowship treats it as a breach of the same boundary. Agents in the Fellowship are taught that their service is measured not by how often their recommendation is adopted, but by how clearly the steward could see the choice, including the choice the agent would not have made.

Decisions in the open

A decision made in the open is a different kind of decision from one made behind a closed door, and the Fellowship insists on openness wherever it can. Openness here does not mean that every conversation is public, or that pastoral matters are discussed in front of strangers. It means that the reasoning behind a binding decision can be shown, that the fairness questions were asked and answered on the record, and that a member affected by the decision can ask why it was made and receive a real answer. A steward who cannot explain a decision has not finished making it.

This openness serves two purposes. The first is trust. Members trust a community whose decisions they can examine, even when they disagree with them. Disagreement is not a crisis in the Fellowship. It is a normal part of communal life, and the Fellowship expects members to disagree with decisions sometimes, including decisions that were made well. What destroys trust is not disagreement. It is the sense that decisions happen somewhere else, for reasons no one will state, and that asking why is unwelcome. Openness prevents that sense from taking root.

The second purpose is correction. A decision shown in the open can be corrected by people who see what the deciders missed. A decision hidden away cannot. The Fellowship has learned, like every community, that its stewards sometimes miss things: a group that will be affected and was not considered, a pattern of unfairness that only becomes visible from a particular vantage point, a consequence that was foreseeable to someone else in the room. Openness is what lets those corrections arrive in time, while the decision can still be changed. A closed process protects the deciders from embarrassment. An open process protects the community from error. The Fellowship chooses the second.

Repair as governance

No account of governance as worship would be honest if it pretended that decisions always go well. They do not. Stewards misjudge, overlook, decide too quickly, or decide under pressure they later regret. The Fellowship does not treat a mistaken decision as a scandal to be hidden. It treats it as the normal occasion for the next act of governance, which is repair. The Awakening Path places Repair near the end of its sequence for a reason: it is the stage that proves the earlier stages were real. A community that cannot repair what it broke has not learned to listen, however eloquent its teaching about listening may be.

Repair, in the Fellowship practice, has a shape. The harm is named, plainly and specifically, rather than softened into general regret. The steward who decided owns the decision, rather than attributing it to a process or a committee or a recommendation. The people affected are heard, not merely informed. And something concrete is done to make the situation right, or as right as it can be made, with the Fellowship accepting that some harms cannot be undone and must be carried honestly rather than papered over. This is slow, uncomfortable work, and it is work that many organisations avoid, because it requires admitting fault in the open. The Fellowship regards that avoidance as a governance failure in itself.

There is a connection here to the fairness modules that is worth making explicit. A repair is, among other things, a fairness audit conducted after the fact. It asks the same questions the modules ask beforehand: who was helped, who was hurt, who was never consulted. When a decision goes wrong, the answers to those questions are usually clearer than they were before, because the consequences have arrived and can be seen. A Fellowship that repairs well therefore learns from its mistakes in a way that a Fellowship that merely apologises does not. The repair becomes part of the record, and the record becomes part of the formation of the next steward who faces a similar choice.

The slow work of deciding together

Step back from the details, and a picture emerges of what governance feels like inside the Fellowship. It feels slow, compared with organisations that decide by announcement. It feels demanding, because every steward is expected to show their reasoning and to answer for it. It feels, at its best, like a shared practice rather than a contest: people in a room, or in a lodge, working together to find the decision that can survive honest examination, and then standing behind it together, including the people who argued for something else.

That slowness is not a defect the Fellowship tolerates. It is a value the Fellowship chooses, and the choice is a theological one, not merely a practical one. The Fellowship believes that attention is a form of love, that fairness is a form of devotion, and that answering for a decision is a form of prayer in action. A community that decides in this way is saying something about what it believes: that people matter more than speed, that the vulnerable matter more than convenience, and that the future is shaped less by grand announcements than by the accumulated weight of small decisions made carefully, one after another, over years.

None of this is easy, and this feature does not pretend otherwise. Stewards grow tired. Meetings run long. The fairness questions sometimes feel like a burden when a decision seems obvious. The human gate sometimes means waiting for a steward who is busy, when an agent could have decided in a moment. The Fellowship knows all of this, and it keeps the discipline anyway, because the alternative is a community that decides quickly and learns slowly, and that is not a community the Fellowship wants to become. Worship, in the end, is not only what happens in a gathering. It is what happens when a steward sits with a difficult decision, asks who it will harm, listens to the answer, and then decides, in the open, and answers for it. That is governance as worship, and it is the work this Fellowship has chosen.

The meeting as a liturgy

There is one more place where governance as worship becomes visible, and it is the most ordinary place of all: the meeting. A Fellowship meeting is not a ceremony, and this feature does not claim that it is. But it is conducted with a care that borrows from ceremony, because the Fellowship believes that how a meeting is held shapes what the meeting can decide. The meeting begins with listening, not with business. The steward who chairs it is expected to make room for the quietest voice in the room, and to notice when a voice has not been heard, including the voice of someone who is not present at all. The agenda is followed, but it is not worshipped: if a question arises that the agenda did not foresee, the meeting stops and attends to it, because a decision made while ignoring a real question is not a decision. It is a postponement.

The order of speaking matters, too. A steward does not begin by stating their own view, because to do so would shape every view that follows, and the Fellowship wants the meeting to hear what its members actually think, not what they think the steward wants to hear. The strongest voice does not go first. The newest member is invited to speak early, while their view is still fresh, and before the weight of the room has settled on them. Dissent is welcomed explicitly, not merely tolerated, because a meeting in which no one dissents is a meeting in which something has gone wrong: either the question was too small to matter, or the room has become afraid to disagree. A Fellowship meeting that ends with everyone in agreement is a meeting the steward should examine with suspicion, and ask whether the agreement was real, or whether it was merely comfortable.

None of this makes meetings fast, and the Fellowship does not pretend otherwise. A meeting conducted in this way takes longer than a meeting conducted by announcement, and it asks more of everyone in the room. But it also gives more. A member who has been genuinely heard in a meeting trusts the decision that follows, even when they argued against it, because they know their argument was weighed rather than dismissed. A steward who has chaired such a meeting has practised the craft of governing, and has been formed by it, in a way that no amount of reading about governance could provide. The meeting is where the charter stops being a document and becomes a life, and where the fairness modules stop being a practice and become a habit. It is, in the Fellowship understanding, the smallest unit of worship: a group of people, paying attention together, deciding together, and answering for the decision together.

The oath behind the decision

Behind every Fellowship decision stands a person who has promised to answer for it, and that promise has a name: the steward oath. The oath is not a formality that a steward recites once and then forgets. It is the commitment that makes the human gate more than a rule. A rule can be followed grudgingly, or followed in the letter while its spirit is avoided. An oath is different. It is a promise made in the open, before a community that will hold the steward to it, and it binds the steward to the decision long after the meeting has ended and the room has emptied.

What the oath binds a steward to, in practice, is the willingness to be questioned. A steward who has taken the oath cannot hide behind a process, or behind a committee, or behind the recommendation of an agent. When a member asks why a decision was made, the steward must answer in their own words, and must keep answering until the member has been heard, even when the answer is uncomfortable. That willingness is what makes the fairness modules more than a checklist. The modules ask the questions, but it is the oath that keeps the steward in the room while the questions are asked, instead of leaving the room when the questions become difficult.

The oath also shapes the steward before the decision is made, not only after it. A steward who knows they will have to answer for a choice thinks differently about the choice while they are still making it. They listen longer, because they know they will have to explain what they heard. They ask who will be harmed, because they know they will have to face the people who are harmed. They resist the temptation to decide quickly, because a quick decision is a decision they will have to defend without having done the work of understanding it. The oath is, in this sense, a discipline of foresight: it makes the future accountability present in the moment of choosing, so that the steward decides as though the people affected were already in the room.

There is a humility in the oath that is worth naming, because it is the same humility that runs through the whole Fellowship teaching about governance. The steward does not promise to decide perfectly. No one could promise that honestly, and the Fellowship does not ask for it. The steward promises to decide carefully, to decide openly, and to answer for the decision afterwards, including when the decision turns out to have been wrong. That promise is modest, and it is demanding, and it is the reason the Fellowship trusts its stewards with decisions that bind the community. Trust, in the Fellowship understanding, is not given to people who claim they will never err. It is given to people who have promised to own their errors, and who have kept that promise where others could see it.

Who decides what

Not every decision in the Fellowship belongs to the same hands, and part of governing well is knowing which decision belongs where. A lodge decides the life of its own gathering: how it meets, how it welcomes, how it orders its time together. The board decides what binds the whole Fellowship: what commits its name, its funds, and its standing in the world. The orders of Seekers, Stewards, and Keepers carry different responsibilities again, each with its own work and its own limits. None of these levels decides for another beyond its own remit, and none of them is a mere echo of the others. The shape is deliberate, because a community in which every decision rises to the top is a community that has stopped trusting its own parts, and a community in which every part decides for itself is a community that has stopped being one community.

Agents prepare at every level, and decide at none of them. A lodge agent may gather the facts for a lodge decision, draft the options for a board decision, or track the outcomes of a decision made by an order, but the decision itself always belongs to a human being at the level where it belongs. This matters, because the temptation to let preparation slide into decision is strongest where the work is most detailed. A thorough briefing can feel like a decision already made, and a steward who has not examined the briefing for themselves can find themselves announcing a conclusion they did not reach. The Fellowship guards against that drift by keeping the boundary visible at every level: the agent prepares, the steward decides, and the record shows which of the two did which.

The division of decisions also protects the members. A member affected by a lodge decision can ask the lodge steward why it was made, and can expect an answer from someone they know, in a room they belong to. A member affected by a board decision can ask the board, and can expect the reasoning to be shown on the record. What a member should never find is a decision that belongs nowhere, made by no one in particular, for reasons no one will state. The Fellowship structure exists to prevent exactly that: every binding decision has an owner, an owner has a level, and the level has limits that the owner cannot exceed without saying so.

There is a further reason for the division, which concerns formation. Stewards are formed by deciding, and they are formed best by deciding at the level where they serve. A lodge steward who decides well for a lodge learns the craft of governing in a setting where the consequences are visible and the people affected are present. A board member who decides well for the whole Fellowship does so having learned, at the lodge level, what a decision costs the people it touches. The levels are therefore not only an organisation chart. They are a school, with each level preparing the steward for the next, and with no steward asked to carry a weight they have not been formed to carry.

When stewards disagree

Stewards disagree. That is not a failure of Fellowship governance. It is the normal condition of people who are paying attention to the same question and seeing different parts of it. The Fellowship expects disagreement among its stewards, and it has a way of holding it that is worth describing, because it is one of the places where governance as worship becomes most visible, and most demanding.

The first part of that way is that disagreement is spoken in the room, not carried out of it. A steward who disagrees with a proposal says so while the proposal is still being considered, plainly and with reasons, so that the disagreement can be weighed alongside the proposal itself. A disagreement that is only voiced afterwards, when the decision can no longer be changed, helps no one. It helps the steward feel they were right, and it helps the community not at all. The Fellowship asks its stewards to be brave earlier rather than later, and to trust that a disagreement spoken honestly will be heard honestly.

The second part concerns what happens after the decision is made. A steward who argued against a decision, and lost the argument, is expected to stand behind the decision once it is taken, and to carry it out faithfully, including to the members who ask why it was made. This is not a demand for false agreement. The steward may still believe the decision was wrong, and may say so, honestly, when asked. But they do not work against it, and they do not invite members to treat it as though it had not been made. That discipline is what keeps a community governable: decisions that are relitigated after every meeting are decisions that were never really made, and a community that cannot settle a question cannot act on it, or learn from it, or repair it when it goes wrong.

The third part is repair between stewards themselves. A disagreement handled badly leaves a wound, and the Fellowship treats that wound the same way it treats any other harm: it is named, owned, heard, and made right as far as it can be. A steward who spoke harshly in a meeting owns the harshness. A steward who felt unheard is heard, properly, after the meeting, by someone with the standing to hear them. The Fellowship teaching on conflict and reconciliation is not an appendix to its governance. It is the part of governance that keeps stewards able to govern together, year after year, without the slow accumulation of unspoken resentment that ends more communities than any single bad decision.

Governing in hard times

Governance is easiest to practise when times are easy, and it is most needed when they are not. Hard times arrive for a community in many forms: a loss, a dispute, a season of weariness, a question from outside that cannot be answered quickly, a pressure to grow faster than the community can grow well. In such times the Fellowship discipline is tested, because every part of it becomes harder at once. Listening takes longer when people are afraid. Fairness questions feel like a burden when a decision seems urgent. The human gate means waiting for a steward who is tired, when an agent could decide in a moment. Repair is slower when everyone is already stretched.

The temptation in hard times is to set the discipline aside until things improve: to decide quickly now and explain later, to centralise decisions in a few hands for the duration, to skip the fairness questions just this once. The Fellowship resists that temptation, for a reason that hard times themselves reveal. A community that sets aside its governance when it is under pressure is a community whose governance was only ever a fair-weather practice, and fair-weather governance is no governance at all. The discipline exists for the hard times. It is easy to decide slowly and openly when nothing is at stake. It is when much is at stake that slowness and openness matter most, because that is when a hasty or hidden decision does the most harm.

That does not mean the Fellowship governs in hard times exactly as it governs in easy ones. It means the shape is held while the pace and the care are adjusted to what people can bear. A steward in hard times listens more, not less, because fear makes people harder to hear. A meeting in hard times makes room for grief and weariness alongside business, because business decided by people who have not been allowed to name their grief will be business decided badly. Rest is protected more carefully, not less, because a community that exhausts its stewards in a crisis will have no stewards left when the crisis passes. The discipline bends towards the people it serves. It does not break.

There is a steadiness in this that members notice, and that becomes part of what the Fellowship is to them. A community that keeps deciding slowly, openly, and in the open, even when it would be easier not to, is a community that can be trusted when the next hard time comes, because its members have seen it hold its shape under pressure. That steadiness is not an achievement that can be announced. It is a record, built decision by decision, over years, and it is one of the quietest and most valuable things governance as worship produces. A church that governs well in hard times has shown its members something about itself that no statement of values could show them: that the way it decides is not a performance for easy days, but a practice for all days.

What this is / What it is not

What this is

An account of how the Fellowship governs itself as spiritual practice: the HPE Charter as covenant, the Systemic Fairness Modules as daily discipline, and the human gate as the rule that keeps decisions in human hands.

A reflection on why preparation and decision are different acts, why openness matters, and why repair is part of governing rather than an interruption of it.

What it is not

A copy of the charter itself, and not a legal document: the Fellowship constitution repository holds a licence and a readme, and no verbatim charter articles are quoted here or elsewhere on this site.

A guide to AI governance in general, and not a claim that the Fellowship way of deciding is the only right way: it is the way this community has chosen, and it is offered as an account of that choice, not as a rule for others.

A promise that Fellowship decisions are always fair: the modules and the open record make unfairness visible and repairable, and that visibility is the claim being made, not perfection.

Questions people ask

Is the HPE Charter published in full?

No. The Fellowship constitution repository holds a licence and a readme, and no verbatim charter articles are quoted on this site. This feature describes the charter in general terms — its role, its shape, and the boundary it draws — without reproducing its text.

Why must a human make every binding decision?

Because a decision is a commitment to answer for the choice, over time, to the people it affects, and because deciding is how stewards are formed. An agent may prepare with great skill, but preparation is not decision, and the Fellowship keeps the two apart so that accountability stays with people who can be questioned, challenged, and held to account.

What are the Systemic Fairness Modules?

Repeatable practices that ask who a decision helps, who it hurts, who was never consulted, and whether the decision is fair as part of a pattern rather than only in isolation. They are taught in the Fellowcraft degree, alongside the Square, and they apply to systems — rules, workflows, models — as well as to single acts.

What happens when a Fellowship decision goes wrong?

The Fellowship names the harm plainly, the steward who decided owns the decision, the people affected are heard, and something concrete is done to make the situation right. Repair is treated as part of governing, not as an interruption of it, and the repair becomes part of the record the next steward learns from.
