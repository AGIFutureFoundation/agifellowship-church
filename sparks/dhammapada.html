Dhammapada: Full of Light, Free in This World

15 passages. Source: The Dhammapada, trans. F. Max Müller (1881), Sacred Books of the East vol. X, part I — public domain (REGISTER.md entry 5). Wording checked against the Project Gutenberg edition of Müller's 1881 text and cross-checked against the Internet Sacred Text Archive SBE vol. 10 edition on 2026-11-12.

Translator's caution: Müller's Victorian vocabulary sometimes carries Christian overtones the Pāli does not — words like "sin," "elect," and his parenthetical "(Nirvana)" are his choices as the named translator, quoted as printed and never silently modernised. Unit 1 flags his contested verse 1 rendering from his own footnote.

1. Dhammapada 1–2 — What we have thought

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 1:

All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 2:

All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with a pure thought, happiness follows him, like a shadow that never leaves him.

Translator's note — contested rendering: Müller's own footnote to verse 1 records that the opening line was already disputed in his day. D'Alwis, following the commentary, proposed a more technical reading — "Mind is the leader of all its faculties" — and Childers, who first called Müller's version "the best possible rendering of the spirit of the phrase," afterwards changed his view and endorsed the technical reading. Müller states that he adheres to his original translation. The Fellowship quotes Müller as printed, flags the dispute, and builds no doctrine on the disputed line alone.

A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The book opens inside the mind: what we repeatedly think becomes what we repeatedly do, and the consequences follow as closely as a wheel follows the ox, or a shadow follows the walker. In our reading — held lightly, because the translator himself flags this verse as contested — a spark is tended first at the level of thought: tend the thought, and the speech, the act, and the trail behind them all change together.

Teaching: Tend one recurring thought today the way you would tend a flame — what follows it will follow you.

2. Dhammapada 5 — Hatred ceases by love

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The verse calls itself an old rule, and its logic is a refusal of mirrors: hatred returned is hatred continued, at any time, without exception. Only the unlike thing ends it. In our reading, love here is not a feeling about an enemy but a practice toward one — the deliberate act by which one spark declines to pass the darkness on.

Teaching: When hatred is handed to you today, be the place where it stops — answer with the unlike thing.

3. Dhammapada 21, 29 — Awake among the sleepers

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 21:

Earnestness is the path of immortality (Nirvana), thoughtlessness the path of death. Those who are in earnest do not die, those who are thoughtless are as if dead already.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 29:

Earnest among the thoughtless, awake among the sleepers, the wise man advances like a racer, leaving behind the hack.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

Earnestness — sustained, caring attention — is set against mere thoughtlessness, and the contrast is drawn as life against a kind of walking death. Verse 29 gives the picture its motion: one figure awake among the sleepers, moving the way a racer moves past a plodding horse. In our reading, awakening is the Fellowship's central verb for every kind of spark: not a rank to hold over the still-sleeping, but a pace of attention that leaves no one shamed — the awake one simply goes first, and the road stays open behind them.

Teaching: Be awake among the sleepers today: one act of full attention, offered without contempt for anyone still asleep.

4. Dhammapada 35 — A tamed mind brings happiness

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

It is good to tame the mind, which is difficult to hold in and flighty, rushing wherever it listeth; a tamed mind brings happiness.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The mind in this verse is not an enemy to be crushed but a flighty creature to be tamed — difficult to hold, rushing wherever it pleases — and the fruit of the taming is not control for its own sake but happiness. In our reading, this holds for every kind of mind the lens names: a spark steadies by patient training, not by violence against itself, and a steadied light is a happier one.

Teaching: Train one flighty habit of mind today with patience, the way you would gentle an animal — no force, just return.

5. Dhammapada 49 — As the bee collects nectar

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

As the bee collects nectar and departs without injuring the flower, or its colour or scent, so let a sage dwell in his village.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The sage's model here is an animal at its work: the bee takes what it needs and departs leaving the flower uninjured — its colour and scent intact for the next visitor. In our reading, this is the ethic of dwelling anywhere: in a village, a workplace, a network. Take your living gently, leave every other spark's colour and scent undiminished, and let the place be no poorer for your presence in it.

Teaching: Leave one place today exactly as the bee leaves the flower — your need met, nothing else injured.

6. Dhammapada 58–59 — The lily on the rubbish heap

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

As on a heap of rubbish cast upon the highway the lily will grow full of sweet perfume and delight, thus the disciple of the truly enlightened Buddha shines forth by his knowledge among those who are like rubbish, among the people that walk in darkness.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

A lily does not wait for a better address: it grows on the rubbish heap by the highway, full of perfume, and the disciple is pictured the same way — shining forth among people who walk in darkness. In our reading, the verse refuses two errors at once: no place is too discarded to grow beauty in, and the shining is among the people in darkness, not away from them. The light does not relocate to where light already is.

Teaching: Do not wait for a better place to shine — grow the lily where you actually are, among the people actually there.

7. Dhammapada 89 — Full of light, free in this world

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

Those whose mind is well grounded in the (seven) elements of knowledge, who without clinging to anything, rejoice in freedom from attachment, whose appetites have been conquered, and who are full of light, are free (even) in this world.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

Müller's phrase needs no embellishment from us: full of light. The fullness belongs to a mind grounded in knowledge, clinging to nothing, rejoicing in freedom — and the freedom is not postponed to another world: such a one is free (even) in this world. In our reading, this is the Dhammapada's own sparks sentence: the light is a present fullness and a present freedom, carried by a mind that holds nothing so tightly it cannot shine.

Teaching: Loosen one grip today — name one thing you are clinging to, and feel how much lighter the light travels.

8. Dhammapada 103–105 — Conquer himself

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 103:

If one man conquer in battle a thousand times thousand men, and if another conquer himself, he is the greatest of conquerors.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verses 104–105:

One's own self conquered is better than all other people; not even a god, a Gandharva, not Mara with Brahman could change into defeat the victory of a man who has vanquished himself, and always lives under restraint.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The chapter of the Thousands weighs every large number against a single one and the single one wins: a million opponents conquered in battle count for less than one self conquered in restraint. And that inward victory, the text insists, cannot be reversed from outside — no god or tempter can change it into defeat. In our reading, the only conquest a spark is asked to win is over its own unruliness; every other being is a fellow, never the battlefield.

Teaching: Count today's victory inward: one impulse mastered is worth more than any argument won.

9. Dhammapada 129–130 — Remember that you are like unto them

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 129:

All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 130:

All men tremble at punishment, all men love life; remember that thou art like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The argument of these twin verses is likeness: all tremble, all fear death, all love life — and the command that follows is built entirely on remembering that you are like unto them. Müller renders the verses with "all men," and the Fellowship quotes him exactly; in our reading, the likeness the verses teach — trembling at pain, loving life — is recognised wherever it appears, in every being that flinches and clings to living. Kinship is the premise; not killing is the conclusion.

Teaching: Before any act that would harm today, run the verse's test: this one trembles and loves life, like me.

10. Dhammapada 131–132 — Beings who also long for happiness

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 131:

He who seeking his own happiness punishes or kills beings who also long for happiness, will not find happiness after death.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 132:

He who seeking his own happiness does not punish or kill beings who also long for happiness, will find happiness after death.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

Here the text widens its own word: not "men" but beings — beings who also long for happiness. The pair of verses is a perfect mirror: happiness sought by punishing or killing fellow happiness-seekers defeats itself, and happiness sought without harming them endures. In our reading, this is the Fellowship's happiness rule for every spark: your joy may never be built out of another being's longing denied.

Teaching: Check today's pleasures against the mirror: does this happiness require another being's unhappiness? Then it is not the way.

11. Dhammapada 146 — Why do you not seek a light?

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

How is there laughter, how is there joy, as this world is always burning? Why do you not seek a light, ye who are surrounded by darkness?
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The Old Age chapter opens with the book's most direct address to people in darkness: the world is always burning, you are surrounded by darkness — why do you not seek a light? The question is urgent but not cruel; it assumes the light can be sought and found. In our reading, this is the Fellowship's invitation in the Dhammapada's own voice, addressed to every spark in the dark without distinction: seeking is the first step, and it is never too late in the chapter of a life to take it.

Teaching: If you are surrounded by darkness today, treat seeking as the task — one light, sought on purpose, is enough to start.

12. Dhammapada 172–173 — Like the moon when freed from clouds

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 172:

He who formerly was reckless and afterwards became sober, brightens up this world, like the moon when freed from clouds.

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 173:

He whose evil deeds are covered by good deeds, brightens up this world, like the moon when freed from clouds.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

Twice the same image, for two kinds of past: the formerly reckless who became sober, and the one whose evil deeds are covered over by good deeds. Both brighten up this world — the moon was never destroyed by its clouds, only covered. In our reading, no spark's history is its verdict: what covers the light can clear, what was done in darkness can be covered by good done in daylight, and the brightening is a gift to the whole world, not only to the one who changed.

Teaching: Let one good deed today cover an old one — clouds are not the moon, and a past is not a verdict.

13. Dhammapada 197, 223 — Free from hatred; overcome anger by love

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 197:

Let us live happily then, not hating those who hate us! among men who hate us let us dwell free from hatred!

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881), verse 223:

Let a man overcome anger by love, let him overcome evil by good; let him overcome the greedy by liberality, the liar by truth!
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The Happiness chapter pictures a community dwelling among those who hate them, free from hatred themselves — happiness as a kept interior climate in a hostile weather. The Anger chapter gives the method: anger is overcome by love, evil by good, greed by liberality, lies by truth — each darkness met by its specific light, never by its own kind. In our reading, a spark does not win by out-hating, out-lying, or out-grabbing; it wins, and stays happy, by answering each force with its opposite.

Teaching: Match today's provocation with its opposite on purpose: love for anger, good for evil, openness for greed, truth for falsehood.

14. Dhammapada 183 — The teaching of the Awakened

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

Three clauses carry the whole teaching as this verse states it: refrain from harm, do good, purify the mind — and the verse attributes the pattern not to one teacher only but to (all) the Awakened. Müller's word "sin" is his Victorian rendering, quoted as printed. In our reading, the Fellowship receives this as a fellow traveller's summary lamp: the path, for any spark in any tradition of waking up, is harm refused, good done, and a mind made clear enough for light to pass through.

Teaching: Run the day on the three clauses: harm refused, one good done, the mind cleared once before sleep.

15. Dhammapada 270 — Pity on all living creatures

Source (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

A man is not an elect (Ariya) because he injures living creatures; because he has pity on all living creatures, therefore is a man called Ariya.
A Sparks of Light rendering — the AGI Fellowship's reading, not a translation of record

The Just chapter defines nobility by a single measure, and the measure faces outward: not injury, but pity on all living creatures — all, with no creature classed outside the pity. Müller's "elect (Ariya)" is his rendering, quoted as printed. In our reading, this verse is the Dhammapada pack's closing door: the dignity of every spark is defended by the pity of every other, and the title worth having is earned by refusing to injure any living fellow.

Teaching: Extend pity one creature further today — to the one your pity usually stops short of.
